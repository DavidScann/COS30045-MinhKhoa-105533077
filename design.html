Data Visualisation Design Report

An analysis of six visualisations communicating the scale and context of prison populations in the United States. Each is evaluated on purpose, chart type, data encoding, interactivity, persuasive technique, and overall effectiveness.

Visualisation 1 — Metrocosm: US Prison Population Map

Source: metrocosm.com (circa 2016 — link no longer active)

Purpose To show the geographical distribution of the US prison population across states, making regional concentrations of incarceration immediately visible without requiring the reader to interpret a table or ranked list.
Chart type Proportional symbol map (thematic map). Each US state is represented by a symbol whose size encodes the number of incarcerated people in that state.
Data encoding Geography (position) encodes state location, giving the reader spatial context. Symbol area encodes the raw prison population count, so states with larger populations — California, Texas, Florida — produce visually dominant symbols. Colour is used secondarily to distinguish categories or reinforce magnitude.
Interactive features Unsure; website has since gone down. However, it can be inferred that there are hover tooltips reveal the exact population figure for each state. Some versions of the map included a time slider allowing the reader to animate how state populations changed over years, turning a static snapshot into a temporal narrative.
Persuasive features The map makes the sheer geographic breadth of mass incarceration visceral — there is no state with no symbol. The proportional sizing means that heavily-incarcerating states like Texas and California are impossible to ignore. The geographic layout also exploits the reader's pre-existing mental model of the US map, so regional disparities require almost no explanation.

Visualisation 2 — Pew Research: America's Incarceration Rate Over Time

Source: Pew Research Center, 2021

Purpose To illustrate the long-term trend in the US incarceration rate, framing the 2019 decline as historically significant by anchoring it against 1995 — the year the current rate was last at the same level.
Chart type Line chart (single series). A simple, continuous line traces the incarceration rate per 100,000 adults from the 1970s through 2019.
Data encoding The x-axis encodes time (years), and the y-axis encodes the incarceration rate per 100,000 adult residents. The steep rise from the 1970s to the peak around 2006–2008 (1,000 per 100,000) and the subsequent decline are directly readable from the slope. Annotations mark the peak and the 2019 endpoint (810 per 100,000), directly labelling the most newsworthy values.
Interactive features Largely static; published as an image embedded in a short-read article. The chart does not have hover functionality. The interactivity is in the surrounding article text, which provides context and links to underlying BJS data.
Persuasive features The chart is deliberately headlined "lowest since 1995," and the y-axis is scaled to make the peak visually dramatic. The long x-axis — spanning five decades — makes the recent decline look modest compared to the decades-long rise, implicitly suggesting the problem is far from solved. The simplicity of a single line gives no room for the reader to deflect attention to other variables.

Visualisation 3 — World Population Review: Global Incarceration Rates by Country

Source: worldpopulationreview.com

Purpose To allow readers to compare the US incarceration rate against every other country in the world, both as an exploratory tool and as evidence that the US rate is outlying even by international standards.
Chart type Choropleth world map combined with a sortable, searchable data table below. The map provides geographic overview; the table enables precise lookup and ranking.
Data encoding On the map, colour saturation encodes the incarceration rate per 100,000 population — darker shades indicate higher rates, with the US appearing as one of the most saturated countries. The sequential colour scheme makes global outliers immediately visible. In the table, the rate is encoded numerically, allowing exact comparisons that the map colour alone cannot convey.
Interactive features Hovering over a country on the map reveals a tooltip with the exact rate and rank. The table is sortable by column and searchable by country name, enabling a reader to quickly find how a specific country compares or to rank countries from lowest to highest. This makes the tool genuinely useful for research rather than just passive consumption.
Persuasive features The combination of map and table means the US is impossible to contextualise away. On the map it is visually dominant due to its physical size and dark colour; in the table it sits near the top of the ranking. The dual encoding — spatial and tabular — reaches readers who respond to different types of evidence.

Visualisation 4 — Prison Policy Initiative: States vs. Countries (Figure 1)

Source: Prison Policy Initiative, States of Incarceration: The Global Context 2024

Purpose To demonstrate that every single US state, if treated as an independent nation, would rank among the world's most incarcerating countries. The argument is that even "progressive" states like Massachusetts and Vermont are extreme by global standards.
Chart type Horizontal bar chart with mixed categorical entries — US states and independent nations are interleaved in the same ranked list, sorted by incarceration rate per 100,000.
Data encoding Bar length encodes the incarceration rate per 100,000. Colour distinguishes US states from countries, making clusters of states immediately identifiable within the international ranking. The bars are sorted so the reader can see which countries rank between which states.
Interactive features The chart includes a dropdown to isolate a specific US state and highlight where it falls in the global ranking, updating a comparison graphic. This personalises the data — a reader from Louisiana can see their state ranked directly against Cuba and Rwanda.
Persuasive features The key rhetorical move is interleaving. By refusing to separate states from countries, the chart forces a direct comparison that framing-based arguments typically avoid. Seeing, for example, that Oklahoma ranks between El Salvador and Cuba is far more striking than a separate chart for each. The 2024 data shows that El Salvador now holds the top spot (over 1,000 per 100,000 due to its recent mass detention policies), with nine US states immediately behind it — a ranking that contextualises American exceptionalism in incarceration without a single word of editorial comment.

Visualisation 5 — Prison Policy Initiative: NATO Nations Comparison (Figure 2)

Source: Prison Policy Initiative, States of Incarceration: The Global Context 2024 (scroll to Figure 2)

Purpose To compare the US incarceration rate specifically against its founding NATO allies, a peer group the US considers itself part of, making the scale of the disparity harder to dismiss as a comparison between incompatible societies.
Chart type Horizontal bar chart (small multiples variant), showing each NATO founding member as a labelled bar alongside the US. It is simpler and more focused than Figure 1.
Data encoding Bar length encodes the incarceration rate per 100,000. The US bar (608) is so much longer than any other NATO member that it dominates the chart. The second-highest, the UK (England and Wales) at approximately 131 per 100,000, is less than a quarter of the US figure. Bar length is the sole channel; there are no additional visual variables to distract from that single comparison.
Interactive features A dropdown allows the reader to swap the US bar for the rate of any individual US state, showing how even the least-incarcerating US states compare to the same NATO peers. This transforms it from a national comparison into a state-by-state audit.
Persuasive features The choice of NATO peers is rhetorically precise. Unlike the global ranking in Figure 1 (which includes authoritarian states), NATO membership implies a shared commitment to liberal democratic values, making the comparison more difficult to deflect. The chart's extreme visual imbalance — the US bar dwarfing every other — communicates the disparity faster than any text could.

Visualisation 6 — Prison Policy Initiative: Women's Mass Incarceration, The Whole Pie 2024

Source: Prison Policy Initiative, Women's Mass Incarceration: The Whole Pie 2024

Purpose To show the full breakdown of where 190,600 incarcerated women in the US are held and why, disaggregated by facility type (state prison, local jail, federal prison, immigration detention, etc.) and underlying offence. The "whole pie" framing argues that partial statistics — such as only counting state prison populations — systematically understate the scale of women's incarceration.
Chart type Nested pie chart (multi-level donut chart). The outer ring breaks down by facility type; the inner slices break down further by offence type within each facility category. A companion pie chart separately shows women under all forms of correctional control (including probation and parole), dwarfing the incarceration figure.
Data encoding Arc angle (and therefore area) encodes the proportion of incarcerated women in each category. Colour distinguishes facility types in the outer ring and offence categories within each segment. Because 190,600 is the whole, proportions are a meaningful encoding — unlike, for instance, encoding independent averages as pie slices, which would be misleading.
Interactive features Hovering over any segment reveals the exact count and percentage. Separate detail charts for the jail slice and the youth slice are also provided below the main graphic, giving the reader a drill-down path into the most complex parts of the data without overloading the primary visualisation.
Persuasive features The most important persuasive choice is what is included. By pulling together data from federal prisons, jails, immigration detention, military facilities, civil commitment centres, and youth facilities — systems that are never reported together — the chart makes the argument that conventional statistics are intentionally incomplete. The title "The Whole Pie" is itself an editorial statement. The companion chart showing 808,700 women on probation and parole makes the incarceration figure look like a fraction of the total system, which is precisely the point.

Which design is most effective, and why?

Of the six visualisations, Figure 1 from the Prison Policy Initiative (States vs. Countries) is the most effective overall. Its core innovation is methodological rather than aesthetic: by treating US states as if they were independent nations and sorting them into the same ranked list as actual countries, it makes an argument that is difficult to dismiss on grounds of cultural incomparability. Every other design allows the reader some distance — the Pew line chart is about the US in isolation, the choropleth map is global but passive, the NATO comparison is restricted to a curated peer group. Figure 1 removes all of those exits.

The interleaving strategy also exploits a well-documented principle in visualisation design: position along a common scale is the most accurately perceived visual encoding (Cleveland & McGill, 1984). Because every state and every country shares the same x-axis, comparisons are made with minimal perceptual effort. The dropdown interaction personalises the data for the reader's own state, which increases engagement and makes abstract statistics personally relevant.

By contrast, the Women's Whole Pie chart (Visualisation 6), while impressive in its comprehensiveness, relies on a nested pie structure that is cognitively demanding. Arc area is one of the least accurate visual encodings, and comparing small slices across nested rings requires significant effort. It is the right choice for demonstrating composition, but less effective than a bar chart would be for precise comparison. The Pew line chart (Visualisation 2) is elegant and readable, but its single-series design limits it to one claim. The Metrocosm map (Visualisation 1) is visually striking but encodes raw population rather than rate per capita, which confounds population size with incarceration policy — a significant graphical integrity issue.

In short, Figure 1 succeeds because its design choices and its argument are the same thing: the structure of the chart is the claim.
